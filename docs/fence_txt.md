<div>
<p>
The original chain link fence came with the house when the alumni bought the house in 1967.
</p>
<p>

As you heard at the last tour stop, In 2016, the house exterior paint and wood rot repair was finished.  The house came back looking the way it was meant to look. 
</p>
<p>

The fence in front of it however did not match.

Ron Machalak put it best. He said the property looked "architecturally naked".
</p>
<p>

Ron compared our house to the other historic houses in the area. They all had a nice fence in front complementing each house.
</p>
<p>

In 2020, Ron began talks with the restaurant owner next door.  The restaurant next door started replacing their own fence as part of their renovation, and that was the catalyst for this project. 

Our fence attaches to theirs.  Our fence picks up where the restaurant's wall leaves off, carries across the front, and ends up the driveway.

If we were ever going to change the fence, that was the moment.
</p>
<p>

The pandemic made all projects go more slowly. But in late 2022, they finally started the demolition of the old and the installation of the new fence.
</p>
<p>


Now came the decisions as to what to install.
We knew we did not want a six foot privacy fence. At six feet, with pointed tops, a fence stops defining a yard and starts defending it.
</p>
<p>

So we chose a four foot height, which you will notice repeats around the area on other houses.
</p>
<p>

For the material, we had three real candidates.

Wrought iron is the most durable and looks the part. It is also the most expensive by a wide margin, and it rusts. That means scraping and repainting on a cycle somebody has to own for the next thirty years.

Powder coated steel looks nearly identical from ten feet away and costs less. But the coating is all that stands between the steel and the weather. One rock chip and rust starts.

Aluminum will not rust at all.
</p>
<p>

There was one more hurdle. This house sits in a historic district, so the fence needed approval from the city's Historical Commission. Ron created the project, led the project, and walked it through the Commission himself.
</p>
<p>

The old chain link came out. The new posts went in forty two inches deep. That's below the frost line. A 12 inch concrete strip runs along the base of the entire fence, which keeps the weeds out and keeps the whole run maintenance free. The old gate was replaced with a matching aluminum gate.
</p>
<p>

111 linear feet, finished in December of 2022.

The fence cost $14,400. That's considerably more than Ron expected when he started.

$9,600 of that came from a handful of individual alumni donors. The alumni association treasury covered the rest.
</p>
<p>

So when you look down this run of fence, roughly two thirds of what you are seeing was paid for by alumni who decided it mattered.

It is finished, and paid for, and it will last longer than the original fence it replaced.
</p>
<p>

At the end of this tour, you will get a link to the Google Drive containing photos of the construction of the fence, including shots like the concrete forms.
</p>
<p>

Now we have a beautiful, durable, front fence that matches the wonderful new exterior paint on the house.
</p>
<p>

Not every stop on this tour ends that way. Some of what you will see today is still unfinished.

</p>
